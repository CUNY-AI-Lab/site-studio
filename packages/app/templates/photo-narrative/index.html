The Last Shoemaker

A portrait of Mr. Giuseppe Romano and 60 years of craft

Photographs and text by Jamie Lee

↓

Every morning at 7:30 AM, Giuseppe Romano unlocks the door to Romano's Shoe Repair, a small shop on Mulberry Street that has been in his family for three generations. At 82 years old, he is one of the last traditional shoemakers in Little Italy.

Giuseppe learned the craft from his father, who learned it from his father before him. "When I was a boy in Italy," he says, "everyone knew how to fix things. Nothing was thrown away. You repaired, you mended, you made things last."

His hands move with practiced precision, stitching leather that most would consider beyond repair. Each movement is deliberate, economical, the result of tens of thousands of hours at the bench.

Tools of the Trade

The tools Giuseppe uses belonged to his grandfather. Some are over a hundred years old— hammers worn smooth by generations of hands, awls sharpened countless times, wooden lasts shaped to fit feet long gone.

"These tools," he says, running his fingers over a curved knife, "they knew my grandfather's touch, my father's touch, and now mine. When I'm gone, who will remember how to use them?"

The neighborhood has changed dramatically in Giuseppe's lifetime. The Italian families who once filled these streets have mostly moved away. The shops his parents knew— the butcher, the baker, the tailor—have all closed, replaced by boutiques and restaurants that cater to tourists.

But Giuseppe's shop remains, a small island of the old neighborhood in a sea of change. Locals still bring him their shoes, their bags, their belts—items too good to throw away but damaged beyond what most repair shops will handle.

"People don't understand the value of repair anymore. Everything is disposable now. But a good pair of shoes, properly cared for, can last a lifetime."

— Giuseppe Romano

Giuseppe never married, never had children. The shop has been his life. He arrives early, leaves late, working six days a week. "What else would I do?" he asks with a shrug. "This is who I am. This is what I know."

He knows the shop will close when he retires. There's no one to pass it to, no apprentice learning the old ways. "Maybe that's how it should be," he says. "Times change. People change. But while I'm here, I'll keep fixing shoes."

As the afternoon light filters through the dusty shop windows, Giuseppe bends over his work, stitching, hammering, shaping. The sounds of his craft—the tap of hammer on leather, the rasp of file on rubber—have echoed in this space for nearly a century.

One day, perhaps soon, those sounds will fall silent. But for now, Giuseppe Romano continues his work, a keeper of old knowledge, a practitioner of a dying art, the last shoemaker on Mulberry Street.

About This Project

The Last Shoemaker was photographed over six months in 2024. I visited Giuseppe's shop weekly, documenting his daily routine and the rhythms of his craft. This project was supported by a grant from the New York Foundation for the Arts.

Jamie Lee is a documentary photographer based in New York City. Their work focuses on artisans, craftspeople, and disappearing traditions. Previous projects have been published in National Geographic and The Guardian.
