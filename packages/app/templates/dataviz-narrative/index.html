The Climate Migration Story

How rising temperatures are reshaping where Americans live

Data analysis by Jordan Kim | January 2024

Scroll to explore ↓

A Nation on the Move

Over the past two decades, millions of Americans have relocated in response to changing climate conditions. Rising temperatures, increased wildfire risk, and water scarcity are driving migration patterns not seen since the Dust Bowl era.

This data story explores these movements and what they mean for American cities and communities.

Temperature Rise Across the U.S.

Average temperatures have increased significantly across all regions since 2000. The Southwest has experienced the most dramatic warming, with average summer temperatures rising by 3.2°F.

24 million

Americans moved between states from 2010-2020, many citing climate concerns as a factor

Where People Are Leaving

The Southwest and Southeast have seen the largest outmigration numbers. While economic factors play a role, surveys show climate concerns—particularly heat, drought, and hurricane risk—are increasingly important in relocation decisions.

New Destinations

The Pacific Northwest, Upper Midwest, and New England have seen population increases as people seek cooler climates and lower climate risk. Cities like Duluth, Minnesota and Portland, Maine are marketing themselves as "climate havens."

"We left Phoenix after the summer of 2023 when we had 55 days over 110°F. It wasn't just uncomfortable—it felt dangerous. We couldn't let our kids play outside for months."
— Maria Gonzalez, Phoenix → Minneapolis migrant

The Economics of Climate Migration

Climate migration comes with costs. Housing prices in climate-advantaged cities have risen faster than the national average, while property values in high-risk areas face uncertainty. Insurance costs in vulnerable regions have increased by 50-200% since 2015.

Unequal Adaptation

Not everyone can afford to move. Lower-income households and communities of color are more likely to live in climate-vulnerable areas and less able to relocate. This raises profound questions about environmental justice and who bears the burden of climate adaptation.

Federal disaster aid and climate adaptation funding often fails to reach the communities that need it most, creating a cycle of vulnerability.

Looking Ahead

Climate models predict these migration patterns will accelerate. By 2050, an estimated 50-100 million Americans could be living in areas with significantly higher climate risk than today.

The question isn't whether climate will reshape where Americans live, but how we ensure this transition is just and equitable.

Data & Methodology

This analysis combines data from U.S. Census Bureau migration statistics, NOAA climate records, real estate data from Zillow and CoreLogic, and survey data from the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication.

Temperature data is averaged over 5-year periods to smooth year-to-year variation. Migration numbers include both international and domestic moves. All dollar amounts are inflation-adjusted to 2023 dollars.
